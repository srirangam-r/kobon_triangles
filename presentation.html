Shrinking the Kobon Gap

Theorems, solvers and AI referees on the 18-line problem

Can 18 straight lines make 94 non-overlapping triangles? The best anyone has found is 93. We turned the question into one inequality. Then we ran a loop that proves away whole classes of arrangements, turns each proven fact into a SAT constraint, and searches only what is left.

The problem

Count the triangles no line cuts through

Draw n straight lines. A Kobon triangle is a bounded triangular face of the drawing: a triangle with no other line passing through it. The task is to arrange the lines so that there are as many as possible.

  • The hill: submit n lines as integer triples [a, b, c], meaning ax + by + c = 0. An exact rational-arithmetic evaluator counts the faces, and each n has its own leaderboard. The default is n = 18.
  • Why it is hard: there are astronomically many combinatorially different arrangements. Our solver estimates for the full 18-line problem ran to days or weeks.
A 94 needs triple crossings, points where three or more lines meet. Blanc proved in 2008 that 93 is the limit without them. The case with them is open, and every record for even n uses them.

Bounds for 18 lines. About 3,000 distinct 93s are known (the Utkin–Parpalak gallery), and none of them improves to 94.

One identity

Counting sides turns “≤ 93” into one inequality

Each triangle has 3 sides, and every side is a segment: a piece of a line between two consecutive crossings. A segment borders at most two triangles, one on each side. Counting sides both ways gives

3T = S − Z + D
  • S is the number of bounded segments. S = n(n−2) − Σ k(k−2), so each triple crossing removes 3.
  • Z is the number of unused segments, which border no triangle. D is the number of shared segments, which border two.

Rearranged, this is 3T = n(n−2) − Λ, with Λ = Z − D + ΣP kP(kP−2). For 18 lines, n(n−2) = 288, so T ≥ 94 ⟺ Λ ≤ 6. Every known 93 has Λ = 9.

The engine

Blanc’s parity argument

This is the step every theorem here builds on. SAT solvers are known to be weak at parity and counting arguments like this, which is why the theorems do this part of the work.

Our extensions to lines through triple crossings

Cap blocks

A shared segment must have a triple crossing at one end. There, a single cap line is the first to cross three consecutive rays. This bounds D.

Axis lemma

If a triple crossing has two cap blocks, the line through their middle rays still claims an unused segment, by the same parity argument.

Credits (Lemma A)

Next to each cap point, the axis segment is unused unless a mutual pair of triple crossings occurs. That gives one extra unused segment.

Parity through triple points

Lemma D (C11): a line whose number of triple points plus cap roles is even must claim. This version has been refereed as correct.

Two engines

Proofs remove classes. Solvers finish rigid leftovers.

Theorems

  • Remove whole classes of arrangements at once, for example all arrangements with at most 3 triple crossings.
  • Good at parity and counting, which solvers are provably bad at.
  • They stall on rigid, fiddly configurations.

SAT solver

  • Exhaustive search over a combinatorial model of line arrangements.
  • Grinds through case-by-case geometry that no one wants to do by hand.
  • An “impossible” answer comes with a proof log that an independent checker can replay.

Neither alone

  • The solver on the full problem: estimates ran to days to weeks.
  • The theorems alone stall at a handful of tight patterns.
  • Together: the proofs shrink the space, and the solver searches only the small, constrained remainder.

The loop now

Decoupled: generate, verify and solve in parallel

Refereeing was the bottleneck, so it now runs asynchronously. A generator agent keeps proposing lemmas, a verifier agent referees them in its own time, and the orchestrator encodes claims into the solver as soon as they arrive.

  • Ledger. Each claim has one status: proposed → encoded → sampled → refereed-correct, refereed-fixed or refuted.
  • Tags. Every solver constraint carries the ID of the claim it came from, so a refuted claim rolls back cleanly.
  • Why this is safe: an unrefereed claim can only make the solver miss a 94, never invent one. Any SAT answer is checked exactly. Every UNSAT result is conditional on the tags it used until those claims are refereed.

Who does what

Orchestrator

The main agent. It plans, derives, encodes claims, runs and monitors the solver, applies the stop rule, and keeps the record honest.

Generator

A math subagent. It proposes lemma Cn with a proof and a suggested SAT form, and never referees its own claims.

Verifier

An independent, sceptical referee. It writes its own test code, checks each lemma on exact arrangements, and fixes or refutes claims.

Compute

A 24-core laptop runs Kissat and CaDiCaL. AutoLab gave the official leaderboard score.

The SAT model

How an arrangement becomes a SAT problem

Variables

Sort the lines by slope. For every triple i < j < k, one three-valued sign records whether the crossing of lines i and k lies above, on or below line j. “On” means the three lines meet at one point. That is 816 signs for 18 lines, and they determine the whole arrangement.

Consistency

In 60,000 random 4-line arrangements, including forced concurrent ones, exactly 17 sign patterns occur. Every 4 lines must show one of them. This is a relaxation: every real arrangement satisfies it, so “impossible” carries over to straight lines.

Derived structure

Crossing order: on line r, i comes before j exactly when the sorted sign (r, i, j) is −1. Adjacency comes from “nothing in between”. A triangle means the three crossings are consecutive on all three lines, which is the evaluator’s rule. Target: at least 94 triangles.

The stop rule

Before any long run, solve a random sample of cases and extrapolate. Queue order misleads, because easy cases come first. We do not start runs projected past about 12 hours. Stopping is a result too: both big stops ended in hand proofs that took minutes.

Where could a 94 hide?

The space, class by class

Statuses come live from the claims ledger. “Refereed” means an independent AI referee checked the argument and tested its lemmas on exact arrangements.

Other evidence: no 94 is one line away from any of the 3,016 known 93s (54,288 delete-and-reinsert cases, solved exactly). No 94 comes from adding a line to a perfect 17-line arrangement (a DP over all 255 wiring diagrams). “Bridge” (β) means a shared segment joining two triple crossings.

Separate the author from the checker

What the referees caught

The referee agents were told to break the proofs, and they found real errors. Every fix went back into both the write-up and the solver constraints.

Unrefereed claims still go into the solver, but tagged. A refuted claim would be rolled back, and so far none has been refuted outright. Several were fixed: the statement was narrowed or the proof repaired.

Two triple crossings, case B

The proof assumed cap blocks never overlap. The referee built explicit counterexamples on the ray toward the other point, then gave a simpler correct argument. The solver deductions that depended on the assumption were deleted.

Per-line parity rule (C6)

My statement was false: there were counterexamples among real 18-line 93s. The corrected rule is “K + jalt odd” with explicit hypotheses, which matches C11.

Budgets (C9) and the 4-point pattern (C8)

“At most 2 cap blocks per point” fails at centroid-type points. The budgets survive with a new argument. C8 holds only without bridges, so the solver restriction was scoped to β = 0.

7+ triple crossings (C7) and mutual pairs (C15)

A bent-point bound in Lemma C was wrong: an exact 7-line example reaches c = 2. The impossibility conclusions still hold. In C15, “the partner point is type X” is false (284 counterexamples), but the corollary used for k = 6 survives.

The solver right now

Six triple crossings, no bridges: closed by the solver

C14 shows that a 94 with 6 triple crossings and no bridges needs Z = 0, 12 cap blocks, and every line that avoids the triple crossings acting as a cap line. That is the most constrained instance so far: k6z.

  • Encoded: “every segment is used” (Z = 0), β = 0, cap lines, the Lemma A clause (C10), at least one shared line, and line 0 off every triple crossing.
  • 1.5M variables and 8.1M clauses.
  • Pilot: 8 of 8 random depth-4 cases were UNSAT, in 1–233 s. The whole instance at once timed out at 300 s.
  • C16 and C17 fix every line end at infinity, which gives a complete split into 4,118 cases (search/wedge_cubes.py).
  • C27 kills 3,636 of the (A) cases at once. The easy half of the rest contradicts the 180° symmetry break. With C26 encoded, the 16 remaining (A) cases are UNSAT in 5–24 s and DRAT-verified. (B) is killed by hand: C28/C29 .
  • The verifier's audit caught one real bug: an id collision in the (B) solver run. Those results were discarded and the generators fixed.

Status

Where this stands

Honest status

  • Refereed: no 94 with ≤ 4 triple crossings, none in general position, and none with 5 or 6 triple crossings and no bridge. Theorem G holds for every even n.
  • “Refereed” means independent AI referees plus exact testing. There has been no human expert review and no Lean formalisation yet.
  • Also closed (refereed claims, audited encodings, DRAT-verified solver steps): 5 and 6 triple crossings with no bridge.
  • Open: 5 or 6 triple crossings with a bridge, 7 or more, and 4-fold points on shared lines.

Lessons

  • Read first, compute second. The decisive idea came from a 2008 paper.
  • Proofs remove classes; solvers finish rigid leftovers.
  • Separate the author from the checker. The referees caught what the author missed.
  • Decide in advance when to stop. Sample, extrapolate, then commit or kill.
  • Tag every constraint, so an unrefereed claim is cheap to use and cheap to undo.

What “done” looks like

  • The residual cases are closed by hand or by solver runs with DRAT proofs. Combined with the refereed theorems, that gives a complete proof that 94 is impossible.
  • Then: a Lean check of the counting and parity core, and expert review.
